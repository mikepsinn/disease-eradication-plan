# The Wrong Question

## How to Evaluate This Proposal

When you read this plan, your brain will do something unhelpful. It will ask: "Can the person who wrote this pull it off?"

This is the wrong question. It is understandable. It is also the reason your species has been dying of curable diseases for six thousand years.

The right question is: **Can 8 billion humans, 2,800 billionaires, millions of scientists, thousands of institutions, and 195 governments — the same species that split the atom, sequenced the human genome, eradicated smallpox, put a robot on Mars, and connected every human brain on the planet via pocket rectangles — persuade 180 politicians to act in their rational self-interest for five minutes?**

That is the feasibility question. Not whether one person with a PDF can do it. Whether *your entire civilization* can do it, given the blueprint, the math, and a financial incentive that makes doing the right thing the most profitable trade in human history.

Let me restate what "it" is, because your brain also tends to catastrophize the ask:

- **"It" is not world peace.** It is a 1% budget reallocation.
- **"It" is not curing all diseases.** It is funding clinical trials that are currently unfunded.
- **"It" is not convincing politicians to be moral.** It is paying them to vote yes, which is how they already make every decision.
- **"It" is not unprecedented.** You have signed 560+ multilateral treaties. You banned chemical weapons, biological weapons, landmines, cluster munitions, and atmospheric nuclear testing. You coordinated a global phaseout of ozone-destroying chemicals in 14 months. One woman with a phone convinced 122 countries to stop burying explosives where children walk. Five people in a Geneva office got 122 nations to vote for a nuclear ban and won a Nobel Prize.

You did harder things than this before lunch. You just did them for worse reasons.

### The Evaluation Framework

When evaluating any proposal for systemic change, there are exactly two questions:

**Question 1: If implemented, would it work?**

This is a physics and economics question. Does redirecting money from low-return spending to high-return spending produce better outcomes? Yes. This is arithmetic. The RECOVERY trial proved pragmatic trials work at 82x lower cost. Military-to-health reallocations have produced economic booms every time they've been tried (post-WWII, post-Cold War). The numbers are in the papers, with confidence intervals, and they have not been successfully challenged.

**Question 2: Is implementation achievable?**

This is the question your brain converts into "can this guy do it?" Stop that. Convert it back.

The question is whether **the set of all humans** can coordinate a 1% reallocation when:
- The financial returns are 272% annually for investors
- The political returns are reelection for politicians who vote yes
- The public health returns are 10.7 billion lives
- The economic returns are 30x income growth in 20 years
- The precedent returns are 560+ existing treaties
- The campaign cost is $1 billion (less than a single day of global military spending)
- The coordination threshold is 3.5% of humanity (280 million people)
- Over 10 billion humans downloaded TikTok

You're not evaluating whether one person can end war and disease. You're evaluating whether a species that downloads TikTok by the billions can click a different button when the button pays them.

### What You're Actually Betting On

Here are the five premises. Say "no" to any of them out loud and listen to how it sounds:

1. **Is improvement physically possible?** Governments have cut military spending by 87% before. They've signed hundreds of treaties. They've redirected billions to health before (PEPFAR saved 25 million lives). Yes.

2. **Do the benefits compound?** Healthier people work more, earn more, pay more taxes, fund more research, which produces more cures. Yes. This is compound interest applied to not dying.

3. **Do politicians respond to money?** The defense industry spends $100M/year on lobbying and gets $886B back. The ROI on political spending is 1,813:1. Yes.

4. **Do rich people prefer living?** 2,800 billionaires, each with a 100% chance of dying from disease. At least one prefers not to. Yes.

5. **Can the plan reach one willing billionaire?** Six degrees of separation. The chain reaction model shows it reaches an implementer within 5 years even at 90% dismissal rate. Yes.

You just said yes five times. You cannot simultaneously hold all five yeses and conclude this is impossible. The conclusion is not hope. It is arithmetic.

### The Person Who Wrote This Is Irrelevant

I mean this precisely. The plan does not require its author to implement it. The plan requires:

- One billionaire to fund the bonds (out of 2,800)
- One lobbying firm to pivot (out of thousands)  
- One country to sign first (out of 195)
- 280 million humans to click a button (out of 8 billion who downloaded TikTok)

If the author gets hit by a bus tomorrow, the PDF still exists. The math still works. The incentives still align. The prize mechanism still selects for the best implementation. The only thing that changes is who presents it at the pitch meeting, and pitch meetings are a solved problem.

Isaac Newton did not personally cause apples to fall. He described why they fall. The description was useful regardless of Newton's personal apple-dropping capabilities. This plan describes why war and disease end. The description is useful regardless of the author's personal war-and-disease-ending capabilities.

Evaluate the mechanism, not the messenger.
