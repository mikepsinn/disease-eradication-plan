"Getting one hundred and ninety-five countries to agree costs billions and takes forever."

No. Getting countries to *implement* things costs billions. Getting them to *sign papers* is surprisingly cheap. Humans love signing papers. It makes them feel important.

What Treaties Actually Cost.

Non-governmental organization, or N.G.O., campaign budgets are rarely disclosed publicly, because nonprofits guard their finances the way dragons guard gold. But we know enough to establish something surprising: treaty campaigns run on pocket change.

The Ottawa Treaty banning landmines took fourteen months. It started with one staff member, Jody Williams, who grew a coalition to over a thousand organizations across sixty countries. Canada committed one hundred million Canadian dollars over ten years; international donors added one hundred and sixty-nine million dollars in nineteen ninety-seven for mine action. One hundred and twenty-two states signed. She won the Nobel Peace Prize.

The Cluster Munitions Convention took about two years. A coalition of N.G.O.s backed by a core group of seven governments got ninety-four states to sign and over a million stockpiled munitions destroyed.

The Arms Trade Treaty took a decade but started with just three supporting governments. The Control Arms coalition, led by Oxfam and Amnesty International, published over fifty reports and built support until one hundred and thirty countries signed.

The Nuclear Ban Treaty also took a decade of campaigning. The International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons, or I.C.A.N., ran the entire operation with five staff in a Geneva office, coordinating four hundred and sixty-eight partner organizations. One hundred and twenty-two states voted yes. Another Nobel Peace Prize.

Pattern: One woman with a phone convinced one hundred and twenty-two countries to ban landmines. Five people in a Geneva office won a Nobel Prize. The N.G.O. costs are a few million dollars per year. Government conference costs add ten to thirty million dollars. Total to get a treaty signed: fifteen to fifty million dollars. Your species spends more than that on Super Bowl ads for beer.

How to Make Governments Sign Things.

Politicians move when they see (a) voters demanding it and (b) political cover. Give them both:

First, prove public demand: get three point five percent of the population (two hundred and eighty million people) to say "yes" with verified identity.

Harvard researcher Erica Chenoweth found that every nonviolent campaign with three point five percent plus participation succeeded.

That's not activism. That's a mandate.

Two hundred and eighty million verified humans is the kind of number that makes politicians suddenly remember they work for you.

Second, prove it's safe: peer-reviewed modeling showing every nation gains more in health savings than it loses in military spending.

Politicians need cover the way vampires need darkness. Give them studies to wave around.

Historical Precedents (The Depressing Table).

For the zero point seven percent official development assistance, or O.D.A., target, the goal was zero point seven percent of gross national income, or G.N.I., to foreign aid; only about twenty percent of countries actually did it over fifty-plus years, primarily Scandinavia and Germany.
The Kyoto Protocol set binding emissions targets from nineteen ninety-seven to twenty-twelve; about fifty-five percent of countries initially complied before a collapse where the U.S. never ratified and Canada withdrew.
The Paris Agreement involved non-binding pledges from twenty fifteen to the present; about fifteen to twenty-five percent of the one hundred and ninety-five signatory countries are on track, and perhaps only forty actually meant it.
The NATO two percent G.D.P. defense target has reached compliance for about seventy-two percent of members as of twenty twenty-four; it was only about thirty-two percent until twenty fourteen, when an actual land war in Europe motivated countries to spend more on killing.
The E.U. Stability Pact has aimed for a three percent deficit and sixty percent debt since nineteen ninety-seven but is routinely violated, with even France and Germany breaking their own rules.

Pattern: International financial commitments achieve less than twenty-five percent meaningful compliance. Your species signs things and then doesn't do them. It's like a New Year's resolution, but for geopolitics.

Why a one percent Treaty is Harder Than Most Precedents.

Let's be honest about the obstacles. This is not a chapter written by someone trying to sell you something. (It is. But at least it's honest about the hard parts.).

Touches military budgets: Politicians would rather discuss their browser history than their defense budget.

Requires ongoing annual allocation: Not a one-time signature like banning a weapon. It's a gym membership for peace. Monthly. Forever.

No immediate security threat: Unlike NATO spending increases post-Ukraine. Hard to motivate governments without an active catastrophe, even though one hundred and fifty thousand deaths per day apparently doesn't qualify as one.

Novel mechanism: Military-to-health reallocation has never been done. Humans are suspicious of things they haven't tried before, which is odd for a species that also invented bungee jumping.

Coordination problem: Benefits require critical mass. Nobody wants to go first. Everybody wants to go second.

Why a one percent Treaty Might Beat the Odds.

One. Self-funding: Creates health dividends that exceed the "cost." You make money doing this. That's unusual for moral obligations.

Two. Bipartisan appeal: "Should we cure more diseases?" polls well across every political spectrum. Shocking, I know.

Three. Referendum pathway: Can bypass government resistance through direct democracy. Politicians hate this one weird trick.

Four. Visible beneficiaries: one hundred and fifty thousand deaths per day. Hard to argue with the body count.

Five. Network effects: Each signing country makes the next more likely. Peer pressure: humanity's most reliable technology.

Why The Treaty Won't Stop at one percent

Most international commitments stagnate after signing. You saw the depressing table. Zero point seven percent official development assistance, or O.D.A., targets unmet for fifty-plus years. Kyoto abandoned. Paris missed. Your track record with follow-through makes goldfish look disciplined. So why would a one percent health treaty be any different?

Because this time, people get paid.

The eighty-ten-ten funding structure allocates ten percent of treaty funding (two point seven two billion dollars per year at one percent) to Incentive Alignment Bonds. These are securities that pay out when treaties expand, not just maintain.

In other words: people get rich when more diseases get cured. Finally, a financial instrument that isn't morally bankrupt.

The Expansion Mechanism.

At a one percent treaty level, the annual I.A.B. pool is two point seven two billion dollars per year, creating strong political pressure equivalent to ten times the largest N.G.O. budgets.

At a two percent level, the pool is five point four four billion dollars per year, creating very strong political pressure at a nation-state level.

At a five percent level, the pool is thirteen point six billion dollars per year, creating overwhelming political pressure like a major industry lobby.

At a ten percent level, the pool is twenty-seven point two billion dollars per year, creating unstoppable pressure with no precedent.

Why This Differs from Every Other Treaty.

zero point seven percent official development assistance, or O.D.A., failed because nobody made money when countries complied. The N.G.O.s pushing for compliance have small budgets, compete with each other, and run on guilt. Guilt has terrible fuel efficiency.

The one percent health treaty creates its own expansion lobby. Bond holders profit when the treaty expands to two percent, then five percent, then ten percent. These aren't activists you can ignore at a dinner party. They're investors with quarterly earnings calls about curing diseases.

The Ratchet Effect.

Once one percent passes:

Two point seven two billion dollars per year flows to impact acceleration bond, or I.A.B., holders pushing for two percent.

When two percent passes, five point four four billion dollars per year pushes for five percent.

When five percent passes, thirteen point six billion dollars per year pushes for ten percent.

The political pressure for expansion *accelerates* with each success.

Most treaties are gym memberships purchased in January and abandoned by February. I.A.B.s are the opposite: the constituency for expansion *grows* with each success. Greed doesn't take holidays.
