"Getting one hundred ninety-five countries to agree costs billions and takes forever.".

No. Getting countries to implement things costs billions. Getting them to sign papers is surprisingly cheap. Humans love signing papers. It makes them feel important.

What Treaties Actually Cost.

Non-governmental organization, or N.G.O., campaign budgets are rarely disclosed publicly, because nonprofits guard their finances the way dragons guard gold. But we know enough to establish something surprising: treaty campaigns run on pocket change.

For the Ottawa Treaty on landmines in 1997, the timeline was fourteen months (October 1996 to December 1997). The International Campaign to Ban Landmines, or I.C.B.L., started with one staff member (Jody Williams) and grew to over one thousand organizations in sixty countries. Canada provided a one hundred million Canadian dollar Landmine Fund over ten years, and international donors provided one hundred sixty-nine million dollars in 1997 for mine action. The outcome was that one hundred twenty-two states signed and the campaign won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1997.

For Cluster Munitions in 2008, the timeline was approximately two years (February 2007 to December 2008). The Cluster Munition Coalition, or C.M.C., of N.G.O.s worked with a Core Group of seven governments. Norway funded the United Nations Institute for Disarmament Research, or U.N.I.D.I.R., research project from 2004 to 2008 and hosted Oslo conferences. The outcome was that ninety-four states signed and one point four million stockpiled munitions were destroyed.

For the Arms Trade Treaty in 2013, the timeline was ten years (2003 to 2013). Control Arms involved over one hundred organizations (Oxfam, Amnesty) and published over fifty reports. The United Kingdom and Australia led the diplomatic process and U.N. conferences. The campaign started with three supporting governments and grew to one hundred thirty signatories.

For the Nuclear Ban (Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, or T.P.N.W., 2017), the timeline was ten years (2007 to 2017). The International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons, or I.C.A.N., had five staff (three full-time and two part-time) in Geneva coordinating four hundred sixty-eight partner organizations. A Norwegian government grant funded the Geneva office (2011) and Austria hosted conferences. The outcome was that one hundred twenty-two states voted yes and the campaign won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2017.

Pattern: One woman with a phone convinced one hundred twenty-two countries to ban landmines. Five people in a Geneva office won a Nobel Prize. The N.G.O. costs are a few million dollars per year. Government conference costs add ten to thirty million dollars. Total to get a treaty signed: fifteen to fifty million dollars. Your species spends more than that on Super Bowl ads for beer.

Official Development Assistance, or O.D.A. Target. The target was zero point seven percent of Gross National Income, or G.N.I., to foreign aid. About twenty percent of countries actually did it. It lasted over fifty years. Scandinavia and Germany. That's basically it.

Kyoto Protocol. Binding emissions targets. About fifty-five percent initially, then collapse. From nineteen ninety-seven to twenty-twelve. U.S. never ratified. Canada withdrew. Classic.

Paris Agreement. Non-binding pledges. About fifteen to twenty-five percent on track. From twenty-fifteen to present. One hundred ninety-five countries signed. Maybe forty meant it.

E.U. Stability Pact. Three percent deficit, sixty percent debt. Routinely violated. Since nineteen ninety-seven. France and Germany broke their own rule.

Pattern: International financial commitments achieve less than twenty-five percent meaningful compliance. Your species signs things and then doesn't do them. It's like a New Year's resolution, but for geopolitics.

Why a one percent Treaty is Harder Than Most Precedents.

Let's be honest about the obstacles. This is not a chapter written by someone trying to sell you something. (It is. But at least it's honest about the hard parts.).

Touches military budgets: Politicians would rather discuss their browser history than their defense budget.

Requires ongoing annual allocation: Not a one-time signature like banning a weapon. It's a gym membership for peace. Monthly. Forever.

No immediate security threat: Unlike NATO spending increases post-Ukraine. Hard to motivate governments without an active catastrophe, even though one hundred fifty thousand daily disease deaths apparently doesn't qualify as one.

Novel mechanism: Military-to-health reallocation has never been done. Humans are suspicious of things they haven't tried before, which is odd for a species that also invented bungee jumping.

Coordination problem: Benefits require critical mass. Nobody wants to go first. Everybody wants to go second.

Self-funding: Creates health dividends that exceed the "cost." You make money doing this. That's unusual for moral obligations.

Bipartisan appeal: "Should we cure more diseases?" polls well across every political spectrum. Shocking, I know.

Referendum pathway: Can bypass government resistance through direct democracy. Politicians hate this one weird trick.

Visible beneficiaries: one hundred fifty thousand daily disease deaths. Hard to argue with the body count.

Network effects: Each signing country makes the next more likely. Peer pressure: humanity's most reliable technology.

Why The Treaty Won't Stop at one percent

Most international commitments stagnate after signing. You saw the depressing table. zero point seven percent Official Development Assistance, or O.D.A. targets unmet for fifty plus years. Kyoto abandoned. Paris missed. Your track record with follow-through makes goldfish look disciplined. So why would a one percent health treaty be any different?

Because this time, people get paid.

The eighty ten ten funding structure allocates ten percent of treaty funding (two point seven two billion dollars per year at one percent) to Incentive Alignment Bonds, or I.A.B.s. These are securities that pay out when treaties expand, not just maintain.

In other words: people get rich when more diseases get cured. Finally, a financial instrument that isn't morally bankrupt.

Why This Differs from Every Other Treaty.

zero point seven percent O.D.A. failed because nobody made money when countries complied. The N.G.O.s pushing for compliance have small budgets, compete with each other, and run on guilt. Guilt has terrible fuel efficiency.

The one percent health treaty creates its own expansion lobby. Bond holders profit when the treaty expands to two percent, then five percent, then ten percent. These aren't activists you can ignore at a dinner party. They're investors with quarterly earnings calls about curing diseases.

The Ratchet Effect.

Two point seven two billion dollars per year flows to I.A.B. holders pushing for two percent.

When two percent passes, five point four billion dollars per year pushes for five percent.

When five percent passes, thirteen point five billion dollars per year pushes for ten percent.

The political pressure for expansion accelerates with each success.

Most treaties are gym memberships purchased in January and abandoned by February. I.A.B.s are the opposite: the constituency for expansion grows with each success. Greed doesn't take holidays.
