The Elasticity Question.

The elasticity parameter, e equals one point zero, asks the only interesting question here: if you cut military spending one percent, do war costs actually drop one percent too? Or do humans, being humans, find a way to keep killing each other at full price?

e equals zero point two five. The "humans are barely trainable" scenario. Only a quarter of cuts reduce violence.

e equals zero point five. The "humans are somewhat rational" scenario. Historically optimistic.

e equals one point zero. Full match. The baseline assumption. Probably generous given your track record.

e is greater than one point zero. The "curing cancer together is better bonding than threatening each other with missiles" scenario. Suspiciously wholesome.

What You're Assuming (and Where It Gets Shaky).

The G.D.P. Multiplier.

Spend a dollar on weapons, get sixty cents of economic value. Spend a dollar on healthcare, get four dollars and thirty cents back. Your species chose weapons. Every time. For centuries. I've watched you do this for four thousand, two hundred and ninety-seven years and it has never once gotten less baffling.

The Lost Economic Growth component (two point seven two trillion dollars) already captures this difference. It's not double-counted, just consistently ignored.

The Elasticity Assumption.

The treaty requires multilateral cuts, so relative military balance is preserved; if your neighbor also cuts one percent, neither of you is weaker, you're just both slightly less armed, which historically has never been the problem. The Societal Dividend assumes one percent less spending means one percent less war cost.

Reality is messier. Some costs lag by years. Some wars are fueled by boredom rather than budgets. And military spending may occasionally deter a war (though it starts far more than it prevents, which is a bit like crediting your arsonist neighbor for occasionally calling the fire department).
