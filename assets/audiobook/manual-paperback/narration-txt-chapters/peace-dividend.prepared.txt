The Two Numbers.

The Captured Dividend (the money you definitely get): two point seven two trillion dollars in military spending times one percent equals twenty-seven point two billion dollars per year. That's a bank transfer. It happens because the treaty says it happens. No behavior change required.

The Societal Dividend (money you save by being slightly less insane): one hundred and fourteen billion dollars a year. Depends on whether one percent fewer bombs means one percent fewer wars.

The Elasticity Question.

The elasticity parameter (e equals one point zero) asks the only interesting question here: if you cut military spending one percent, do war costs actually drop one percent too? Or do humans, being humans, find a way to keep killing each other at full price?

At e equals zero point two five (the "humans are barely trainable" scenario), only a quarter of cuts reduce violence. At e equals zero point five (the "humans are somewhat rational" scenario, which is historically optimistic), half the cuts translate. At e equals one point zero (full match, the baseline assumption, probably generous given your track record), every dollar cut is a dollar of war cost saved. At e is greater than one point zero, curing cancer together turns out to be better bonding than threatening each other with missiles, which is suspiciously wholesome and therefore the scenario I trust least.

It doesn't matter. Even at e equals zero point two five, the Captured Dividend of twenty-seven point two billion dollars is treaty-mandated. That money moves regardless of whether you manage to be less violent. It's a bank transfer, not a prayer.

What You're Assuming (and Where It Gets Shaky).

The G.D.P. Multiplier.

Spend a dollar on weapons, get sixty cents of economic value. Spend a dollar on healthcare, get four dollars and thirty cents back. Your species chose weapons. Every time. For centuries. The Lost Economic Growth component (two point seven two trillion dollars) already captures this difference. It's not double-counted, just consistently ignored.

The Elasticity Assumption.

The treaty requires multilateral cuts, so relative military balance is preserved. If your neighbor also cuts one percent, neither of you is weaker. The Societal Dividend assumes one percent less spending means one percent less war cost. Reality is messier. Some costs lag by years.

Some wars are fueled by boredom rather than budgets. And military spending may occasionally deter a war, though it starts far more than it prevents (a bit like crediting your arsonist neighbor for occasionally calling the fire department).

How Good Are the Sources?

I'm being honest about confidence levels, which is more than your defense contractors do.

Military spending data from the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, or S.I.P.R.I. (peer-reviewed), is high confidence.

Infrastructure damage data from the Brown and Watson Costs of War is medium confidence.
