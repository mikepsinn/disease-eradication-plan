The Two Numbers.

The Captured Dividend (the money you definitely get): two point seven two trillion dollars in military spending times one percent equals twenty-seven point two billion dollars per year. That's a bank transfer. It happens because the treaty says it happens. No behavior change required.

The Societal Dividend (money you save by being slightly less insane): one hundred and fourteen billion dollars per year. Depends on whether one percent fewer bombs means one percent fewer wars.

The Elasticity Question.

The elasticity parameter (e equals one point zero) asks the only interesting question here: if you cut military spending one percent, do war costs actually drop one percent too? Or do humans, being humans, find a way to keep killing each other at full price?

At e equals zero point two five (the "humans are barely trainable" scenario), only a quarter of cuts reduce violence. At e equals zero point five (the "humans are somewhat rational" scenario, which is historically optimistic), half the cuts translate.
At e equals one point zero (full match, the baseline assumption, probably generous given your track record), every dollar cut is a dollar of war cost saved.
At e greater than one point zero, curing cancer together turns out to be better bonding than threatening each other with missiles, which is suspiciously wholesome and therefore the scenario I trust least.

It doesn't matter. Even at e equals zero point two five, the Captured Dividend of twenty-seven point two billion dollars is treaty-mandated. That money moves regardless of whether you manage to be less violent. It's a bank transfer, not a prayer.

What You're Assuming (and Where It Gets Shaky).

The G.D.P. Multiplier.

Spend a dollar on weapons, get sixty cents of economic value. Spend a dollar on healthcare, get four dollars and thirty cents back. Your species chose weapons. Every time. For centuries. The Lost Economic Growth component (two point seven two trillion dollars) already captures this difference. It's not double-counted, just consistently ignored.

The Elasticity Assumption.

The treaty requires multilateral cuts, so relative military balance is preserved. If your neighbor also cuts one percent, neither of you is weaker. The Societal Dividend assumes one percent less spending means one percent less war cost. Reality is messier. Some costs lag by years. Some wars are fueled by boredom rather than budgets. And military spending may occasionally deter a war, though it starts far more than it prevents (a bit like crediting your arsonist neighbor for occasionally calling the fire department).

Here's why the elasticity is probably better than one point zero: military spending doesn't just respond to threats; it generates them. Robert Pape at the University of Chicago found that ninety-five percent of suicide terrorist attacks from nineteen eighty to two thousand and three were responses to foreign military occupation, not ideology.

Half were completely secular. Your eight trillion dollar War on Terror took terrorist attacks from about one thousand per year to nearly seventeen thousand by twenty fourteen. Bases provoke resistance, resistance justifies bigger budgets, bigger budgets fund more bases. It's a feedback loop, and feedback loops don't scale linearly.

Cut one percent of the bases, you remove one percent of the provocation, which removes more than one percent of the terrorism, which removes the justification for more than one percent of next year's budget.

The elasticity isn't one point zero because you're not just reducing spending; you're interrupting the cycle that manufactures the thing you're spending on. It's like discovering that your umbrella was causing the rain. (See the full data on occupation and terrorism.).

And you've tested this at scale. After World War Two, America cut military spending eighty-seven point six percent (from one point four two trillion dollars to one hundred and seventy-six billion dollars in today's money) and got the greatest economic boom in human history: eight percent G.D.P. growth for a decade, home ownership doubled, the interstate highway system.

The Cold War ended, spending dropped by half, and you accidentally invented the internet. Every time your species stops buying explosions, prosperity shows up uninvited. You're asking for one percent. You've proven you can handle eighty-seven point six percent.

The Trajectory, Not the Snapshot.

Skeptics ask whether one percent fewer bombs means exactly one percent fewer wars. This is the wrong question. It's like asking whether the first seed planted produced exactly one tree's worth of shade. The question is what happens when the seeds keep growing.

Your species cut military spending eighty-seven point six percent after World War Two and got the greatest economic boom in history. That's the empirical answer. But even that misses the point, because the one percent treaty doesn't stay at one percent.

Incentive Alignment Bonds are tradable securities held by rich and powerful people whose income scales directly with treaty expansion. At one percent, bondholder payouts are two point seven two billion dollars a year. At two percent, they double. At ten percent, they collect ten times as much.
These aren't activists you can ignore at a dinner party. They're investors with Bloomberg terminals, lobbyists, and the same tools your defense contractors currently use to expand military budgets, except pointed at curing diseases.

At just one percent, the I.A.B. political incentive pool (two point seven two billion dollars a year) already outguns your entire defense industry's lobbying budget (one hundred and twenty-seven million dollars a year) by twenty-one point four times. Every expansion multiplies this.
You've built a defense lobby in reverse, and it's twenty-one point four times stronger than the original on day one.

Two Futures.

Your species currently spends eleven point four trillion dollars a year on war. Over fifty years at current levels, that's five hundred and sixty-eight trillion dollars.
This is the price tag of the current trajectory: terrorism feedback loops getting worse (Robert Pape showed bases create the terrorism they're built to stop), an A.I. weapons race that a planet called Moronia already ran to completion, and a species that spends forty-two trillion on self-destruction when twenty-five trillion would have cured everything.

The other future: bondholders push for treaty expansion because their income depends on it. Each expansion weakens the terrorism cycle (fewer bases, fewer attacks, less justification for more bases). The A.I. gets trained on curing cancer instead of targeting civilians.
The money compounds in the right direction.

Over fifty years, the trajectory change is worth one hundred and seventy-five trillion dollars: thirty-three point eight trillion dollars redirected to curing diseases, plus one hundred and forty-one trillion dollars your species no longer spends on the industry of blowing things up and rebuilding them.

That's at e equals one point zero (one percent spending cut equals one percent war cost reduction).

Given that military spending generates the terrorism it's built to fight, the actual elasticity is probably better, which means these numbers are conservative.

Moronia's politicians were rewarded for buying weapons. Moronia's contractors were rewarded for selling weapons. Moronia's A.I. read the receipts and optimized accordingly. Every budget vote was an instruction, and the A.I. was a very good student.

The Incentive Alignment Bureau, or I.A.B., ratchet flips the incentive structure using the same force that currently points it toward explosions: money. The difference between Moronia and the civilization that survived wasn't intelligence, resources, or goodwill.

It was whether the richest people on the planet made more money from expanding the treaty or from letting it die.

The one percent treaty isn't the peace dividend. It's the down payment on a future so nice that your brain is literally incapable of even imagining it currently.
