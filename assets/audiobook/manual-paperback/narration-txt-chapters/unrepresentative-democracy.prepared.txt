The Mathematics of Why Nobody Fixes Anything.

Concentrated Benefits vs. Diffuse Costs.

On Wishonia, we discovered this problem six thousand years ago. It goes like this:

Sugar subsidies cost Americans three point five billion dollars per year, which is ten dollars per person.

Will you take a day off work, drive to your capitol, and stand in a hallway to save ten dollars? No. Will sugar companies spend millions to keep their three point five billion dollars? Obviously.

This is the entire explanation for why your government does what it does. One sentence. Everything else is details. The details are where the crime hides. On Wishonia, we call this "beige crime": theft so boring that nobody investigates it.
Your best criminals discovered long ago that the boredom threshold of the average citizen is approximately ten dollars. Steal more than that from one person and they'll complain. Steal ten dollars from three hundred and thirty million people and you'll receive an industry award.

The pattern:

Pharma companies: five hundred billion dollars in concentrated profits.

Patients: fifteen hundred dollars each in diffuse suffering.

Result: Pharma gets what pharma wants.

Banks in two thousand and eight: seven hundred billion dollar bailout (concentrated).

Taxpayers: twenty-one hundred dollars each (diffuse).

Result: Banks get what banks want.

The Death Spiral: How Two Choices Kill Three Hundred and Thirty Million Options.

A human named James Surowiecki demonstrated that crowds outperform experts under four conditions: diversity of opinion, independence of judgment, decentralization, and a good aggregation mechanism. Your democracy has three of these. It systematically destroys the most important one.

Independence means each person decides based on their own knowledge, not by watching everyone else. Your voting system annihilates this. You have three hundred and thirty million people, many of whom would make excellent leaders. But you don't choose among them.
You choose between two candidates pre-selected by a process optimized for fundraising, name recognition, and not offending people who vote in primaries.

Then, even between those two, you don't vote for the one you like. You vote against the one you fear. Voters who prefer a third candidate vote for a major-party candidate they dislike to avoid "wasting" their vote.
This is called "strategic voting," and it means your ballot doesn't measure what you want. It measures what you think other people will do.

That is not independence. That is a herd.

On Wishonia, we observed this same phenomenon in army ants.

Army ants are nearly blind. They navigate by following pheromone trails laid by the ants ahead of them. When a foraging column gets separated from the main trail, the leading ants have nothing to follow, so they wander. But they're still laying pheromone as they walk.
If the front of the column curves enough to cross its own trail, the leaders pick up that signal and follow it. The circle closes. Every ant is now both following and reinforcing the path that traps it. They march in a loop, sometimes hundreds of thousands strong, until they die of exhaustion.

No individual ant is stupid. Each is following a perfectly rational rule: follow the strongest signal. The system is stupid. The circle emerges not from bad ants but from a structure where no ant can navigate independently.

Your two-party system is a death spiral with stickers. Each voter follows the signal of viability rather than their own judgment. Pollsters tell you who's "electable." Media covers the two "real" candidates. Donors fund the two "viable" options.

You vote for one of them because everyone else is voting for one of them because everyone else is voting for one of them.

The best candidate out of three hundred and thirty million people could be standing right there. But if they're not wearing the right color tie, they're "throwing their vote away," which is a phrase that only makes sense in a system designed to waste votes.

The result: a nation of three hundred and thirty million, selecting from a talent pool of three hundred and thirty million, reliably produces a choice between two people that approximately nobody is enthusiastic about.

Each ant follows the one ahead. The leader follows the last. They march in a circle until they die. Your two-party system works the same way, but the circle takes four years. (Clemzouzou sixty-nine, C.C. B.Y. S.A. four point zero).

How Money Buys Your Government.

Campaign contributions don't directly buy how politicians vote. Only one in four studies support that. The system is much more elegant than bribery. On most planets, this would just be called bribery. On Earth, it's called "campaign finance," because giving it a different name apparently makes it legal.

What money actually buys is which politicians exist in the first place.

Ninety-five percent of House races since two thousand and four were won by the highest spender.

The average winning House campaign costs two point seven nine million dollars.

The average winning Senate campaign costs twenty-six point five three million dollars.

Why bribe a politician when you can manufacture one who already agrees with you? On Wishonia, we had a saying for this: "why train the dog when you can buy a dog that already sits?".

For the ones already in office, contributions buy something more useful than votes:

Access means your call gets returned while everyone else gets voicemail.

Agenda control ensures your issue gets a hearing while everyone else's gets a filing cabinet.

Legislative language provides the exact wording that creates the exact loopholes you need.

Committee positions cost four hundred and fifty thousand dollars to party committees to buy a seat where bills live or die.

You don't buy the vote. You buy the voter, the agenda, and the room where the vote happens. Then the vote takes care of itself.

Public Choice Theory (Or: Why It Doesn't Matter Who You Elect).

In nineteen eighty-six, a human named James Buchanan won what is technically not a Nobel Prize (see central banks chapter) in Economics for formally proving something that anyone who has visited your Department of Motor Vehicles already knew:

Humans do not become better humans when you give them a government ID badge.

This is Public Choice Theory. Bureaucrats, politicians, and regulators are exactly as selfish as everyone else. The only difference is that when a corporation is greedy, you can stop buying its product. When a government agency is greedy, it can arrest you for not buying its product.

The N.I.H. is supposed to provide cures, but it focuses on grants awarded, resulting in no cures.

The F.D.A. is supposed to save lives, but it focuses on avoiding blame, resulting in deadly delays.

Congress is supposed to look out for voter welfare, but it focuses on campaign donations, resulting in corporate welfare.

Pharma is supposed to improve health outcomes, but it focuses on recurring revenue, resulting in chronic disease being preferred.

Defense contractors are supposed to provide national security, but they focus on government contracts, resulting in endless wars.

Nobody in this system is incentivized to cure disease or end war. You could elect five hundred and thirty-five clones of your most idealistic human and within two years they'd be raising money from pharmaceutical companies, because the system requires it and the system does not care who you put inside it.
It digests idealists the way a stomach digests food. Different inputs. Same output.

The Paradox.

Libertarians say: Government fails because government is inherently bad. (Solution: less government.).

Socialists say: Government fails because it needs more resources. (Solution: more government.).

Public Choice Theory says: Government fails because the people running it are people. (Solution: design systems where selfish behavior accidentally produces good outcomes.).

On Wishonia, we went with the third option four thousand, two hundred and ninety-seven years ago. It took us twelve minutes to agree. You've been arguing about the first two for several centuries.

The Ratchet.

The ratchet only turns one way.

After World War Two, the U.S. cut military spending by eighty-seven point six percent in two years. G.M. went from building bombers back to building Cadillacs. The economy boomed. It was the greatest period of broad-based prosperity in your country's history, and it happened immediately after you stopped spending money on killing people.

The military-industrial complex made sure that never happened again.

In inflation-adjusted dollars, peacetime spending is now thirty point six times the pre-World War Two baseline: twenty-nine billion dollars in nineteen thirty-nine versus eight hundred and eighty-six billion dollars today, in constant dollars, with no world war to justify it. The tumor grew its own blood supply and then reclassified the growth as vital organs.

The Equilibrium.

Cures are a one-time payment. Treatments are recurring revenue. Approving a bad drug ends careers. Delaying a good one ends lives, but not careers, and the people making the decisions have careers, not diseases.

Everyone who decides things is winning. Everyone who's dying is not deciding things. The system works perfectly. It is just optimized for the wrong species.
