Unrepresentative Democracy

Princeton proved that public opinion has zero effect on U.S. policy. Corporations spend four point four billion dollars on lobbying and get an eighteen to one return. Your vote is worth zero point zero zero zero one seven dollars. Here's how to make the math work for medicine instead of missiles.

Your chance of deciding a presidential election: one in sixty million. You're more likely to die in a car crash driving to the polls. The expected value of your vote is zero point zero zero zero one seven dollars. The cost of casting it (time, gas, parking) is about fifty dollars.

Democracy was designed so that your individual participation is mathematically meaningless. What isn't meaningless: the four point four billion dollars corporations spend annually on lobbying, according to Open Secrets twenty twenty-four. That math works out just fine.

The Founding Fathers were brilliant men. They anticipated tyranny, corruption, foreign invasion, and mob rule. They did not anticipate Florida.

What Democracy Should Look Like versus Reality.

Princeton University analyzed one thousand seven hundred and seventy-nine policy decisions over twenty years. The correlation between what ordinary voters want and what actually happens? Zero. Percent.

When economic elites want something? seventy-eight percent of the time.

You have the same political influence as your houseplant. Slightly less, actually. Your houseplant produces oxygen, which at least one congressman needs.

Your institutions tell the whole story in their names. You have a "Department of Defense" that mainly attacks people. A "Department of Health" that has not yet produced any observable health. And a "Department of Justice" that (you can see where this is going).

Whether zero percent or one hundred percent of voters support a bill, it has the same roughly thirty percent chance of passing. Democracy is a slot machine where every pull gives the same result but you keep playing because the machine has an American flag on it.

The Mathematics of Political Failure.

Concentrated Benefits versus Diffuse Costs.

Sugar subsidies cost Americans three point five billion dollars per year. That's ten dollars per person.

Will you spend weeks fighting to save ten dollars? No.
Will sugar companies spend millions to keep their three point five billion dollars? Yes.

Guess who wins?

This pattern repeats everywhere:

Pharma companies see five hundred billion dollars in concentrated profits.

Patients suffer one thousand five hundred dollars each in diffuse suffering.

The winner is not you.

Banks in two thousand and eight received a seven hundred billion dollar concentrated bailout.

You paid two thousand one hundred dollars in diffuse taxes.

The winner is the ones with yachts.

The Death Spiral: How Two Parties Kill Crowd Wisdom.

James Surowiecki demonstrated that crowds outperform experts under four conditions: diversity of opinion, independence of judgment, decentralization, and a good aggregation mechanism. Your democracy has three of these. It systematically destroys the most important one.

Independence means each person decides based on their own knowledge and values, not by watching what everyone else is doing. First-past-the-post voting annihilates this. You have three hundred and thirty million people, many of whom would make excellent leaders. But you don't choose among them.

You choose between two candidates pre-selected by a process optimized for fundraising, name recognition, and not offending the party base. Then, even among those two, you don't vote for the one you prefer. You vote against the one you fear.

Voters who genuinely prefer a third-party candidate vote for a major-party candidate they dislike to avoid "wasting" their vote. This is called strategic voting, and it means the ballot doesn't measure what you want. It measures what you think other people will do. That's not independence.

That's a herd.

Army ants have a behavior called a death spiral. Army ants are nearly blind. They navigate entirely by following pheromone trails laid by the ants ahead of them. When a foraging column gets separated from the main trail, the leading ants have nothing to follow, so they wander.

But they're still laying pheromone as they walk. If the front of the column curves enough to cross its own trail (or the back of the column), the leaders pick up that pheromone and follow it. The circle closes. Now every ant is both following and reinforcing the chemical trail that traps it.

They march in a loop, sometimes hundreds of thousands strong, until they die of exhaustion. No individual ant is stupid. Each is following a perfectly rational rule: follow the strongest pheromone signal. The system is stupid.

The circle emerges not from bad individuals but from a structure where no ant can navigate independently.

Your two-party system is a death spiral with "I Voted" stickers. Each voter follows the signal of viability rather than their own judgment. Pollsters tell you who's "electable." Media covers the two "real" candidates. Donors fund the two "viable" options.

You vote for one of them because everyone else is voting for one of them because everyone else is voting for one of them.

The best candidate out of three hundred and thirty million people could be standing right there, but if they're not wearing the right color tie, they're "throwing their vote away," which is a phrase that only makes sense in a system designed to waste votes.

The result: a nation of three hundred and thirty million people, selecting from a talent pool of three hundred and thirty million people, reliably produces a choice between two people that approximately nobody is enthusiastic about. This is the ant death spiral with a larger circle and better catering.

Each ant follows the one ahead. The leader follows the last. They march in a perfect circle until they die. Your two-party system works the same way, but the circle takes four years.

How Money Buys Power.

Campaign contributions don't directly buy how politicians vote. Only one in four studies support that. The system is much more elegant than bribery. Bribery is what poor countries do. Rich countries have "campaign finance.".

But they do something far more effective: they buy which politicians exist in the first place.

Ninety-five percent of House races since two thousand and four were won by the highest spender.

The average winning House campaign costs two point seven nine million dollars.

The average winning Senate campaign costs twenty-six point five three million dollars.

Why bribe a politician when you can just manufacture one who already agrees with you? It's like complaining about the menu when you own the restaurant.

For the ones already in office, contributions buy something more subtle than votes:

Access means your call gets returned, while theirs goes to voicemail.

Agenda control means your issue gets a hearing, while theirs gets buried.

Legislative language provides the exact wording that creates loopholes.

Committee positions can be bought, as four hundred and fifty thousand dollars to party committees buys a seat where bills live or die.

You don't buy the vote. You buy the voter, the agenda, and the room where the vote happens.

The Lobbying R.O.I.: eighteen to one Returns.

The most profitable investment in America isn't stocks, real estate, or crypto. It's congressmen:

Disease advocacy groups spend one hundred million dollars on lobbying.

They get one point eight billion dollars in increased funding.

The R.O.I. is an eighteen to one ratio (one thousand seven hundred percent net return).

Meanwhile:

Breast cancer has great lobbyists, which leads to massive funding.

Heart disease kills more people, but because they have worse lobbyists, they receive less funding.

Rare pediatric diseases have no lobbyists and therefore get no funding.

Democracy doesn't allocate resources by who's dying. It allocates by who can afford the best lobbyist. If your disease can't hire a P.R. firm, it's not a real disease. It's just dying.

Your Congressman: A Fundraiser Who Occasionally Legislates.

Congressmen spend four hours a day fundraising.

They spend zero hours a day reading bills.

Actual governing is squeezed into whatever's left.

They don't read what they vote on. The PATRIOT Act was three hundred and forty-two pages, voted on forty-five minutes after release. That's seven point six pages per minute. Olympic speed-readers can't do that. The Affordable Care Act was two thousand seven hundred pages; "we have to pass it to see what's in it".

These people control nuclear weapons. Sleep tight.

Congressional Committees: The Menu.

Committees control which bills live or die. Both parties assign fundraising quotas for each seat: raise this much for the party, or lose your assignment. Don't hit your number? Your bills die, your amendments vanish, and your name goes on a literal "wall of shame". It's like a restaurant where the waiters have to pay for their own sections.

Here's the menu. Want to be House Speaker? Raise twenty to thirty-one million dollars for your party. Want to chair a powerful committee like Ways and Means or Appropriations? That'll cost you about one point two to one point eight million dollars in party fundraising. Even a basic freshman member owes a six-figure fundraising quota just to keep their seat at the table.

The House Speaker position costs thirty-one million dollars for the D.C.C.C. and twenty million dollars for the N.R.C.C. because that person controls the entire agenda.

The Ways and Means Chair costs one point eight million dollars for the D.C.C.C. and one point two million dollars for the N.R.C.C. because all tax laws start here.

The Appropriations Chair costs one point eight million dollars for the D.C.C.C. and one point two million dollars for the N.R.C.C. because that role controls a one point seven trillion dollar budget.

The Energy and Commerce Chair costs one point eight million dollars for the D.C.C.C. and one point two million dollars for the N.R.C.C. because they oversee healthcare, energy, and telecom.

A Regular Committee seat costs between five hundred thousand and one million dollars for the D.C.C.C. and eight hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars for the N.R.C.C., which still beats irrelevance.

A Basic Member pays one hundred and fifty thousand dollars or more for the D.C.C.C., while the N.R.C.C. cost varies as the entry fee to play.

This is why congressmen spend four hours a day fundraising. They're not campaigning. They're paying rent. The people writing your healthcare laws literally bought their positions from the industries those laws regulate. On your planet, this is called "the system working.".

Public Choice Theory: The Nobel Prize for Cynicism.

In nineteen eighty-six, James Buchanan won the Nobel Prize in Economics for formally proving what anyone who has ever been to the D.M.V. already knew:

People don't become angels when you give them a government I.D. badge.

This is Public Choice Theory: bureaucrats, politicians, and regulators are JUST AS SELFISH as everyone else. The only difference is that when a corporation is greedy, you can stop buying their product. When a bureaucracy is greedy, they can arrest you for not buying their product.

What They Actually Maximize.

The N.I.H. should maximize cures, but it actually maximizes grants awarded, resulting in no cures.

The F.D.A. should maximize lives saved, but it actually maximizes avoiding lawsuits, resulting in deadly delays.

Congress should maximize voter welfare, but it actually maximizes campaign donations, resulting in corporate welfare.

Pharma should maximize health outcomes, but it actually maximizes recurring revenue, meaning chronic disease is preferred.

A Defense Contractor should maximize national security, but it actually maximizes government contracts, resulting in endless wars.

Nobody in the system is incentivized to cure disease. Or end war. You could elect five hundred and thirty-five clones of Gandhi and within two years they'd be taking pharma money. The system digests idealists like a stomach digests food. It doesn't matter what you put in. You get the same output.

The Libertarian Paradox.

Libertarians say: Government fails because government is government.

Socialists say: Government fails because it needs more government.

Public Choice Theory says: Government fails because the people running it are people, and people are terrible.

The solution: Stop expecting people to be good. Design systems where being selfish accidentally produces good outcomes. Like capitalism, but for not dying.

Regulatory Capture: How Industries Write Their Own Rules.

There are thirteen thousand seven hundred registered lobbyists in Washington. Half of them used to work in the government they're now bribing, which on your planet is called "a career path" rather than "corruption." Corporations spend four point four billion dollars a year on lobbying.
Divide that by five hundred and thirty-five members of Congress and you get over eight million dollars in professional persuasion surrounding each congressman who earns less than a mid-level dentist. The mystery is not that they're corrupt. The mystery is that any of them aren't.

Citizens United made corporations people who can donate unlimited money through Super PACs. Corporations are people now, which is odd because you can't imprison a corporation, give it chemotherapy, or make it feel shame. Pharmaceutical companies write their own regulations.
The Medicare Modernization Act banned the government from negotiating drug prices.
That's why insulin that costs six dollars to make costs you three hundred dollars. A four thousand nine hundred percent markup. Your street-corner drug dealers find this excessive, and they don't even have shareholders.

The military-industrial complex distributes operations across forty-two states so that cutting any program threatens local jobs in enough congressional districts to make it impossible. It's a tumor that grew its own blood supply.
But the M.I.C. doesn't just lobby for budgets; it produces the threats that justify them. Robert Pape at the University of Chicago found that ninety-five percent of suicide terrorist attacks from nineteen eighty to two thousand and three were driven by foreign military occupation, not ideology. Half the attackers were secular.
Your eight trillion dollar War on Terror took terrorist attacks from roughly one thousand per year to nearly seventeen thousand by twenty fourteen. Military bases abroad create the resistance that becomes the terrorism that justifies the military budgets that fund more bases abroad.
This is regulatory capture with an extra step: instead of just writing your own regulations, you create the conditions that make the regulations seem necessary. Most industries merely buy favorable rules. The defense industry manufactures the emergencies that make the rules look urgent.
The Pentagon has never passed an audit but somehow knows it needs more money (they can't tell you where the last trillion went, but they're very confident about needing the next one). Pharma employs three lobbyists for every member of Congress.
The F.D.A. gets seventy-five percent of its drug review budget from the companies it regulates. For details, see F.D.A.: Unsafe and Ineffective.

And the ratchet only turns one way. After World War Two, the U.S. cut military spending by eighty-seven point six percent in two years. G.M. went from bombers back to Cadillacs. The economy boomed. But the military-industrial complex made sure that never happened again.
In inflation-adjusted dollars, peacetime spending is now thirty point six times the pre-World War Two baseline: twenty-nine billion dollars in nineteen thirty-nine versus eight hundred and eighty-six billion dollars today, in constant dollars, with no world war to justify it. The tumor doesn't just grow its own blood supply. It grows and then reclassifies the growth as vital organs.

The system maintains itself with the elegant efficiency of a parasite that keeps its host alive just enough to keep feeding. Cures are a one-time payment; treatments are recurring revenue.
Approving a bad drug ends careers; delaying a good one ends lives (but not careers, and careers are what matter to people with careers). Everyone who matters wins. Everyone who's dying loses. The system works perfectly. It's just optimized for the wrong thing.

The Solution They Don't Want.

Lobbying is just one gear in the machine. The full cost of political dysfunction (military overspend, healthcare administration, regulatory friction, tax complexity, corporate welfare, and the rest) totals four point nine trillion dollars per year, or seventeen percent of G.D.P.
A forensic audit (the "Political Dysfunction Tax") identifies two point four five trillion dollars in recoverable capital per year if U.S. governance merely reached O.E.C.D. median efficiency. You don't need a revolution. You need a reallocation.

But nobody fights to recover it, because the losses are diffuse and the benefits are concentrated. Same pattern as this entire chapter: each corporation gains one hundred million dollars (worth a thousand phone calls, a lobbyist army, and a senator's vacation home).
Each citizen loses thousands (not worth a phone call).

But what if curing disease was more profitable than treating it? What if peace paid better than war? That's what a one percent treaty does. It doesn't fight the corruption machine. It feeds it different inputs. You're going to out-bribe the bribers with math.
