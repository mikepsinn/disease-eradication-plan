The Untapped Therapeutic Frontier

There are nine point five million possible drug-disease combinations. You've tested thirty-two thousand five hundred. That's like searching for your keys in zero point three four percent of your house and declaring them lost. The other ninety-nine point seven percent is sitting right there, untested, because paperwork.

On Wishonia, when we decided to cure disease, we started by counting two things: how many safe molecules we had, and how many diseases existed. Then we multiplied them. Then we tested them. It took fifty years. We're still not sure why it took so long.

Your species has been doing pharmacology for a century. I assumed you'd be further along. You are not further along. After one hundred years, you have tested approximately one-third of one percent of the drug-disease combinations you could test using compounds you already know are safe.

The rest is sitting there, untested, because testing things costs money and money is currently busy being missiles.

Here is the math. It is brief.

Your Tiny Sandbox.

Here is everything humanity considers "medicine" after five thousand years of civilization:

There are twenty thousand prescription products approved in the U.S., which sounds impressive until you realize most are just "Tylenol, but blue."

These products contain only one thousand six hundred and fifty unique compounds, which is fewer ingredients than a decent French bakery uses.

Seven thousand five hundred investigational drugs have passed Phase One globally.

The F.D.A. GRAS list contains six hundred and thirty-five substances that you could eat right now without dying.

Total unique compounds humanity knows are safe-ish: nine thousand five hundred compounds.

That's it. That's your entire medicine cabinet after five millennia.

The Target List (Ways Your Body Breaks).

The International Classification of Diseases, or I.C.D. Ten, lists fourteen thousand ways your meat can malfunction. This is your species' most comprehensive to-do list, and you are not doing it.

After consolidation, there are one thousand trial-relevant diseases worth actually fixing.

The Math You're Ignoring.

Take your nine thousand five hundred safe compounds and multiply by one thousand diseases. This gives you the "Combinatorial Space," which is fancy talk for "stuff you could test tomorrow if you weren't busy filling out forms."

Nine thousand five hundred compounds times one thousand diseases equals nine point five million plausible drug-condition combinations.

What You've Actually Tested.

There are one thousand seven hundred and fifty unique approved drug-disease pairings, which represents your entire medical canon.

Thirty percent of drugs gain at least one new indication after initial approval, while the rest never get a second date.

Let's be generous and say you tested ten times as many things that failed (You did. You learned nothing from them.).

Total tested: thirty-two thousand five hundred relationships.

The Scorecard.

Thirty-two thousand five hundred relationships tested divided by nine point five million possibilities equals zero point three four two percent.

Humanity has explored about a third of one percent of the theoretically testable drug-disease space.

You checked one drawer, closed it, and told the patient the case was cold. The patient is also cold, because the patient is dead.

On Wishonia, if someone explored zero point three four percent of something and declared the job finished, we would gently suggest they continue. On Earth, you publish a paper about the zero point three four percent and apply for a grant to study the remaining zero point three four percent you studied last time.

Is The Untested Stuff Useful?

You might argue, "Maybe the other ninety-nine point seven percent is useless!".

Biology would like a word.

Undrugged Targets: Mapping over three hundred and fifty thousand clinical trials showed that only twelve percent of the human interactome has ever been targeted by drugs.

You're ignoring eighty-eight percent of your own biology.

It's like having a one hundred-room mansion and living exclusively in the bathroom.

Polypharmacology: drugs are messy, they hit multiple targets, and F.D.A.-approved drugs often bind to things you didn't intend.

This means "side effects" are frequently "cures for something else" that nobody bothered to notice.

Sildenafil was developed for chest pain, it failed, its "side effect" became Viagra, then it got repurposed *again* for pulmonary hypertension.

Minoxidil was developed for high blood pressure, its "side effect" was growing hair, and it became Rogaine.

Two drugs, three accidental cures, and zero systematic searches.

You keep finding cures by tripping over them in hallways. Imagine if you opened a door on purpose.

Network Overlap: Diseases cluster on shared biological networks.

Arthritis and Alzheimer's share biological pathways.

Lithium was prescribed for gout in the eighteen hundreds; decades later, it turned out to be the gold standard treatment for bipolar disorder.

Nobody connected the dots because nobody was drawing lines between diseases.

The drug that treats your grandfather's inflammation might fix your grandmother's neurodegeneration.

You won't find out, because testing it would require paperwork that takes longer than Grandma has left.

The Universe is Big. You Are Small.

So far we only counted molecules humanity *already has*. The stuff in your cupboards. If you look at what's chemically possible, it gets embarrassing.

Chemical Space: there are an estimated ten to the twenty-third power to ten to the sixtieth power drug-like molecules.

That's more candidates than there are atoms in your body, or stars in the galaxy, or excuses from your regulatory agencies.

Virtual Libraries: ZINC twenty-two contains tens of billions of purchasable virtual molecules.

You could order them online, but you haven't.

Synthesized: You have made less than ten to the negative fortieth power of possible molecules.

Tested divided by possible is approximately ten to the negative twentieth power to ten to the negative fiftieth power.

Effectively zero. You haven't even started.

This is like exploring Earth by looking at one grain of sand on one beach in New Jersey, then declaring you've "seen the world." You haven't. You've seen a grain of sand. In New Jersey.

Why You Are Still Sick.

I want to be very clear about this: you are not sick because curing disease is impossible. You are sick because you built a system that charges one hundred million dollars to try, and then gave all the money to a different system that builds missiles. The missiles work fine.

Trials Cost Too Much.
A typical Phase Two to Three randomized controlled trial, or R.C.T., costs thirty to one hundred million dollars (median cost per patient forty-one thousand dollars).
At that price, you only test things pharmaceutical companies can patent.
Everything else sits untested.
It's a lottery where the ticket costs one hundred million dollars and only rich molecules are allowed to play.

Herd Mentality.
Trials overwhelmingly test the same few biological targets due to preferential attachment dynamics.
Everyone studies what everyone else studies because that's how you get grants.
It's like if every explorer only went to France because France already had good reviews on TripAdvisor.
Meanwhile, undiscovered continents full of cures sit there, undiscovered.

No Profit.
Most molecules have no patent.
No patent means no one hundred million dollar trial, which means no approval.
Metformin, a diabetes drug from the nineteen-fifties, shows persistent signals for reducing cancer risk and extending lifespan.
It costs pennies.
It's off-patent.
The trial to prove it works for aging (T.A.M.E.) spent years begging for funding because no pharmaceutical company will bankroll a drug they can't monopolize.
The molecule that saves your life has to make money first.
If it can't, it won't.
You die.
Quarterly earnings go up.

Neglect.
If you have a rare disease with no market, you don't get a drug.
Your cells malfunction in an unprofitable way.
The invisible hand of the market has weighed your life against quarterly earnings and found you wanting.
Sorry about your death.
It was a business decision.

How Long to Explore Everything?

At the current pace of three thousand three hundred trials per year, how long would it take to systematically test therapeutic possibilities?

Tier 1: Single Compounds (Most Conservative).

Testing every known safe compound against every major disease:

Nine point five million combinations divided by three thousand three hundred trials per year equals two thousand eight hundred and eighty years.

That's almost two thousand eight hundred and eighty years to explore compounds we *already know are safe*. By that math, we won't finish until the year five thousand.

Tier 2: Combination Therapies (Still Defensible).

Modern medicine increasingly relies on multi-drug cocktails. Oncology, H.I.V., cardiology, psychiatry: all routinely use drug combinations. So the real question isn't "does this one drug work?" It's "which combinations work?" Testing pairwise combinations of safe compounds:

Forty-five point one billion combinations divided by three thousand three hundred trials per year equals thirteen point seven million years.

That's longer than *Homo sapiens* has existed as a species. And that's using only compounds with established safety data, testing only pairs. Not triplets. God help you if you want to try triplets.

Tier 3: The Full Chemical Universe (Illustrative).

Beyond known compounds lies the chemical space of ten to the twenty-third power to ten to the sixtieth power drug-like molecules. At current trial capacity, exploring even the *synthesizable* fraction would take longer than the universe has existed. The universe is thirteen point eight billion years old. You need more time than that. To test pills.
Pills that already exist.

We haven't even started.

But Won't We Run Out of Easy Discoveries?

Some will argue "diminishing returns": the more we search, the harder each new discovery becomes. This assumes we've already picked the low-hanging fruit.

You haven't picked *any* fruit. You're standing outside the orchard filling out an application to enter the orchard. The application is forty-seven pages long. The fruit is rotting.

Diminishing returns only kicks in after you've explored a significant fraction of the space. At less than one percent explored, every trial teaches you something new about human biology, improving your ability to predict what works next. Learning compounds. Depletion is negligible.
You are nowhere near the bottom of the barrel because you have not yet located the barrel.

The math: even with weak learning, diminishing returns only matters after exploring about thirty-seven percent of therapeutic space. For combination therapies, that crossover is over five million years away at current pace (thirty-seven percent of forty-five point one billion combinations at three thousand three hundred trials per year).

You cannot have diminishing returns when you've barely started looking. See the formal analysis for the equations.

The Fix.

The Oxford RECOVERY Trial (COVID-19) proved that large-scale pragmatic trials can run for about five hundred dollars and deliver results in less than one hundred days.

Five hundred dollars versus forty-one thousand dollars. Read those numbers again. One of them is the reason people are dying.

With a decentralized F.D.A., trial capacity increases by twelve point three times:

Nine point five million combinations divided by forty thousand seven hundred trials per year equals two hundred and thirty-four years.

Still a long time, but at least it's a number with fewer digits than a phone book. And the first few decades would capture the highest-value discoveries (the low-hanging fruit that current trial scarcity forces you to ignore while people die in the waiting room).

More importantly, your decentralized F.D.A. builds *permanent infrastructure* for systematic therapeutic exploration. The goal isn't to "finish." It's to stop leaving cures on the table while you form subcommittees to discuss whether to form committees to review the proposal to consider them.

The Bottom Line.

Humanity has empirically tested less than zero point three four two percent of the drug-disease relationships that can be tested using existing safe compounds.

If you include the larger chemical universe, humanity has explored roughly one-quadrillionth to one-quintillionth of what is possible (ten to the power of negative twelve to ten to the power of negative fifty). There are numbers so small that mathematicians get depressed looking at them. These are those numbers.

The overwhelming majority of therapeutic potential remains untouched. Not because cures are impossible. Because trials are too expensive and too slow.

A decentralized, automated pragmatic trial system is the only route to systematically explore the remaining ninety-nine point nine nine nine percent of medical space.

The cures exist. The molecules are sitting there. The patients are willing. The only thing between them is a system that charges one hundred million dollars to introduce them and takes seventeen years to make the introduction. On Wishonia, this would be considered a hostage situation.

On Earth, it is considered "regulation."

Everything you know about medicine is a dot. Everything you could know is the rest of the page. The dot is not growing.
