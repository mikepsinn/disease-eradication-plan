Why This Can Work: The Coordination Problem Has a Price Tag.

Public choice theory (a branch of economics devoted to explaining why nothing good ever happens) predicts that concentrated interests beat diffuse interests. Always. Ten lobbyists who each make ten million dollars from war will outfight ten million patients who each lose ten dollars from disease.

This is mathematically correct and morally disgusting, which is how you know it's real economics.

The current system persists not despite its inefficiency but because of it. Inefficiency is profitable for the people running it.

The part Olson didn't account for: the people getting screwed have ninety times more money. They just can't coordinate.

Concentrated interests like defense and fossil fuels have around five trillion dollars in market cap and spend one hundred million to one billion dollars annually on politics. 

Diffuse beneficiaries, meaning everyone who would benefit from cures, have four hundred and fifty-four trillion dollars in household wealth, but currently spend zero dollars due to a coordination failure.

Having more capital doesn't help if you can't deploy it. That's Olson's whole point and he's right. A ninety to one capital advantage means nothing when each individual's share is too small to motivate action. Coordination costs eat everything.

Incentive Alignment Bonds, or I.A.B.s, are a coordination mechanism disguised as a financial product. They turn political change into an investment. Investors don't need to care about curing disease. They need to care about returns.
