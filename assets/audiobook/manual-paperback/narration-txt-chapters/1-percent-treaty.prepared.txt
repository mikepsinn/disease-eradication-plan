A One Percent Treaty

You have enough nuclear weapons to kill everyone thirteen times. Here's how to settle for twelve point eight seven times and use the savings on medicine. Controversial, apparently.

Your one percent Treaty is a piece of paper that says every signatory nation redirects one percent of its annual military budget to enable any patient to participate in pragmatic clinical trials for the most promising new treatments.

The Math.

The Problem: The world spends two point seven two trillion dollars a year on military forces.

The Hack: If every nation reduces military spending by one percent at the same time, the balance of power remains identical.

No country becomes more vulnerable.

The Prize: That one percent, twenty-seven point two billion dollars a year, is redirected to fund clinical trials for every human disease.

For comparison, twenty-seven point two billion dollars is roughly what Americans spend on Valentine's Day, and what humans spend on pet food every three months.

How It Works.

Funding: Creates a twenty-seven point two billion dollars per year revenue stream for the one percent Treaty Fund

Execution: A network of Decentralized Institutes of Health subsidizes patient participation in a D.F.D.A. (a decentralized F.D.A.), clinical trials that are forty-four point one times cheaper than the current model.

Governance: Global population via Wishocracy, with transparent auditable rules and automated fund allocation.

Transparency: All research, data, and spending published on public ledger.

This Isn't a Trade-Off.

It's like if everyone in your neighborhood spent their life savings on attack dogs, and then called it a "safe community" because all the dogs are equally vicious.

When every nation builds one percent fewer explosion devices at the same time, you get two good things:

You become safer. The global "you might all die by accident" temperature drops one percent. Fewer chances of someone pressing the wrong button, which happens more than you'd think.

You stop dying from stupid things. That twenty-seven point two billion dollars pays sick people to try treatments instead of waiting for death.

Economists call this a Kaldor-Hicks improvement, which is their way of saying "the winners win more than the losers lose, and we've decided that counts." In practice, some defense contractors lose one percent of their contracts. In exchange, they and everyone they love become less likely to die of cancer.
Most people consider this a good trade. Defense contractors are still doing the math.

The Dead Capital Problem (Or: Why Bombs Are the Worst Investment Since Tulips).

When countries "invest" in military hardware, they're not buying assets. They're buying very expensive paperweights that occasionally explode.

A bridge.

Generates economic activity daily (trucks move, people commute, value flows).

Lasts fifty to one hundred years.

Makes everyone richer.

Pretty to look at.

A missile.

Sits in a hole doing nothing.

Costs one point five million dollars a year just to keep it from rotting.

Can only destroy things (including itself).

Makes everyone nervous.

This is what economists call "dead capital": resources trapped in a form that can't generate returns. It's like converting your house into a pile of TNT. Impressive, but you can't live in it, rent it out, or do anything except eventually blow it up, at which point you have neither a house nor TNT.

As President Eisenhower (a five-star General who knew about killing professionally) put it:

"Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed, those who are cold and are not clothed."

He literally ran the biggest military in history, and even HE thought you were being ridiculous about this.

A one percent treaty doesn't ask you to spend twenty-seven point two billion dollars curing diseases. It asks you to redirect twenty-seven point two billion dollars from the least useful one percent of your military budget to things that make you richer and less dead.

The Blueprint.

Treaties are pieces of paper that countries sign when they want to pretend they'll behave. Sometimes they work (you haven't had World War Three yet). Sometimes they don't (you've had about two hundred other wars).
But they're the only tool you have for getting countries to do anything together besides kill each other.

Robert Pape at the University of Chicago analyzed every suicide terrorist attack from nineteen eighty to two thousand and three and found that ninety-five percent were responses to foreign military occupation, not religious ideology.

Half were completely secular.

Your eight trillion dollar War on Terror killed nine hundred thousand people and global terrorist attacks went from about one thousand per year to nearly seventeen thousand per year by twenty fourteen.

Your military bases aren't protecting you from terrorism.

A one percent treaty addresses the real threats. It doesn't weaken your ability to handle the conventional ones. You keep ninety-nine percent of your weapons. If ninety-nine percent of your military can't handle the job, the last one percent wasn't going to save you.

Besides, you've done far bigger cuts before. After World War Two, the U.S. slashed military spending by eighty-seven point six percent in two years (from one point four two trillion dollars to one hundred and seventy-six billion dollars in today's dollars). Frigidaire stopped making machine guns and returned to refrigerators. Nobody held a congressional hearing about whether the refrigerator industry could survive without the machine gun contracts. Frigidaire just made refrigerators again. It wasn't complicated.

It was only complicated later, when the machine gun industry hired lobbyists. The economy didn't collapse. It produced the greatest boom in your history.

Today you're spending thirty point six times the pre-World War Two baseline in inflation-adjusted dollars: twenty-nine billion dollars in nineteen thirty-nine versus eight hundred and eighty-six billion dollars today, during peacetime, with no existential threat requiring it.

Your defense industry learned how to make peacetime feel like wartime (see Regulatory Capture). You're asking to redirect one percent of that.

The Money Flow.

The One Percent Treaty Fund.

twenty-seven point two billion dollars annually from military budgets flows into the One Percent Treaty Fund. An eighty-ten-ten automatic split divides it before any funds reach discretionary spending:

Pragmatic Clinical Trials receive eighty percent, or twenty-one point eight billion dollars, for patient subsidies, R and D, and pandemic prep.

VICTORY Incentive Alignment Bond Returns receive ten percent, or two point seven two billion dollars, for perpetual investor payments.

Incentive Alignment Bond Political Incentives receive ten percent, or two point seven two billion dollars, for rewards for supporting legislators.

Notice what this does: it disentangles money from influence. In current government budgets, the people who provide the money (lobbyists, donors) also control where it goes. Money buys influence, influence redirects money, and the loop is invisible. Here, investors get money (ten percent).
Politicians get career incentives (ten percent). Neither group touches the eighty percent that goes to clinical trials. The corruption is capped, transparent, and separated from the public goods funding.

The eighty percent for pragmatic clinical trials is insulated from investor and political capture.
Patients direct trial-participation spending by joining studies, while Wishocracy governs infrastructure and other shared medical public goods, not by a committee of twelve people in a conference room who haven't had a new idea since nineteen eighty-seven.
Every human still gets direct say on the part that actually requires collective choice. Yes, even Florida Man.

What Happens If This Actually Works.

The Endgame.

A one percent treaty isn't the goal. It's the proof of concept. I've watched roughly eight hundred and forty-seven civilizations reach this exact fork in the road; the ones still around all started with something like one percent, and the ones that didn't are geological features now.
Once your species gets a taste of "spending money on things that help instead of things that explode," you'll want more.

The Ratchet Effect.

Public sentiment helps: once cures start rolling out, people want more. "Grandma's dementia is manageable now. Want to try another half-percent?" But your species has wanted cures for centuries without funding them. Popular demand alone doesn't move budgets. Money does.

That's what the Incentive Alignment Bonds are for. Bondholders receive ten percent of all treaty inflows, perpetually. At one percent, that's two point seven two billion dollars a year split among them. At five percent, it's thirteen point six billion dollars. At full redirection, two hundred and seventy-two billion dollars.
Every percentage point increase is a direct raise for every bondholder. These aren't random investors. They're billionaires, family offices, and institutional funds, people who already have senators on speed dial, media access, and campaign donation budgets.
They didn't buy influence over the treaty fund. They bought a revenue share.
But their pre-existing influence over everything else means any politician who tries to shrink or stall the treaty is picking a fight with the richest, most politically connected humans on the planet, all of whom have a quarterly earnings statement explaining why the treaty should be bigger, not smaller.

The defense lobby built a permanent machine to keep military budgets growing. I.A.B.s build the mirror image: a permanent class of wealthy investors whose income scales directly with treaty expansion.
Perpetual bonds mean a perpetual financial incentive to lobby for expansion, every single year, forever. Except these lobbyists have better math and a more popular product ("not dying" polls well).

And the politicians on the receiving end of that pressure? They have their own math. The treaty's ten percent political incentive allocation scales with treaty size.

At one percent, that's two point seven two billion dollars a year funding campaign support and post-office career rewards for legislators who voted yes. At five percent, it's five times that. A politician who votes to expand the treaty is voting to expand their own campaign war chest and retirement options.

A politician who votes against expansion is voting to shrink them. Your species designed a system where politicians follow the money. This just points the money somewhere useful.

More cures lead to more popular support. More bondholder income leads to more political pressure. More treaty funding leads to more campaign support for yes-voting politicians. Three ratchets, one direction.

The endgame: countries bragging about their cancer survival rates instead of their missile counts.

Avoiding Treaty Pitfalls.

Treaties fail in predictable ways. They all fail for the same boring reasons. Here's how this one avoids the common killers:

Reservation Games.

Countries love to sign treaties then add exceptions that gut them. It's like joining a gym but reserving the right to never go.

Solution: No reservations permitted. All or nothing. Take it or leave it.

Ratification Delays.

Sign with fanfare, take a photo, then let it die in committee forever. Your species calls this "diplomacy."

Solution: Provisional application. Takes effect on signature. Ratification just formalizes what already started.

Creative Accounting.

Reclassify "military" spending as "peacekeeping" or "homeland security" to dodge obligations. This is beige crime: making theft so procedurally dull that nobody investigates.

Reclassifying "military" as "peacekeeping" is the bureaucratic equivalent of stealing someone's car by renaming it "shared mobility infrastructure."

Solution: incentive alignment bonds, or I.A.B.s, make creative accounting self-defeating. Politicians who benefit from treaty compliance have personal financial incentive to ensure their nation's full one percent contribution flows through.

Reclassifying military spending to dodge obligations reduces I.A.B. returns for that nation's own legislators, the same people who control budget classifications.

Parliamentary Obstacles.

Executive signs, legislature refuses to implement.

Solution: The treaty obligates the percentage; Congress implements via the annual National Defense Authorization Act (N.D.A.A.). After Medellin v. Texas (two thousand and eight), the Supreme Court made clear that not all treaties are self-executing, so the treaty doesn't try to bypass Congress.
It creates a binding international obligation that Congress fulfills through its normal appropriations process, the same way NATO's spending commitments work. The incentive-alignment bond, or I.A.B. mechanism ensures legislators have a personal incentive to include the one percent line item every year.

Summary.

A one percent treaty is a piece of paper that says "Let's spend one percent less on killing and one percent more on not dying.".

That's it. That's the whole thing.

It doesn't end war. It doesn't destroy the military. It doesn't create world peace.

It just moves the FIRST one percent of your murder budget to your survival budget.

If you can't agree on that, if you can't agree that spending one penny less on death and one penny more on life is a good idea, then you deserve whatever's coming.

WHEREAS, humanity spends two point seven two trillion dollars annually on methods of killing itself;

WHEREAS, this seems somewhat counterproductive;

WHEREAS, diseases kill more people than all wars combined and don't even have the decency to be quick about it;

WHEREAS, we have nuclear weapons sufficient to end civilization thirteen times but haven't cured Alzheimer's once;

WHEREAS, this is embarrassing;

NOW, THEREFORE, the undersigned nations agree to try a different approach, as follows:

Article One: Each signatory shall redirect exactly one percent of its annual military budget to the one percent Treaty Fund for allocation to pragmatic clinical trials.

Article Two: Transfers shall be automatic, immediate, and irrevocable. No "we'll get to it later.".

Article Three: Percentages may increase but never decrease. This is a ratchet, not a yo-yo.

Article Seven: Citizens have standing to enforce via domestic courts. You can sue your own government for non-compliance.

Article Eight: Withdrawal requires unanimous consent of all parties plus ten-year notice. Good luck with that.

Article Nine: This treaty supersedes all conflicting domestic law. Yes, even that law.

Article Ten: Entry into force upon signature by two states. You only need two countries brave enough to go first.

IN WITNESS WHEREOF, the undersigned, being of sound mind and tired of watching their loved ones die of preventable diseases, have executed this Treaty.
