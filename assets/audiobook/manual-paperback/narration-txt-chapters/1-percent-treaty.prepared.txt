Your one percent Treaty is a piece of paper that says every signatory nation redirects one percent of its annual military budget to enable any patient to participate in pragmatic clinical trials for the most promising new treatments. 

The Math.

The Problem: The world spends two point seven two trillion dollars a year on military forces.

The Hack: If every nation reduces military spending by one percent *at the same time*, the balance of power remains identical. No country becomes more vulnerable.

The Prize: That one percent, a twenty-seven point two billion dollar a year river of cash, is redirected to fund clinical trials for every human disease.

For comparison, twenty-seven point two billion dollars is about what Americans spend on Valentine's Day (flowers and chocolate over curing cancer, apparently), and roughly what humans spend on pet food every three months. Apparently too much to ask for curing diseases.

How It Works.

Funding: This creates a twenty-seven point two billion dollar per year revenue stream for the one percent Treaty Fund.

Execution: A network of decentralized institutes of health (D.I.H.s) subsidizes patient participation in a decentralized F.D.A. (D.F.D.A.), clinical trials that are forty-four point one times cheaper than the current model.

Governance: A Decentralized Autonomous Organization (D.A.O.) is governed by smart contracts and the global population via Wishocracy.

Transparency: All research, data, and spending are published on a public ledger.

This Isn't a Trade-Off.

It's like if everyone in your neighborhood spent their life savings on attack dogs, and then called it a "safe community" because all the dogs are equally vicious.

When every nation builds one percent fewer explosion devices *at the same time*, you get two good things:

**You become safer.** The global "you might all die by accident" temperature drops one percent. Fewer chances of someone pressing the wrong button, which happens more than you'd think.

**You stop dying from stupid things.** That twenty-seven point two billion dollars pays sick people to try treatments instead of waiting for death.

Economists call this a Kaldor-Hicks improvement, which is their way of saying "the winners win more than the losers lose, and we've decided that counts." In practice, some defense contractors lose one percent of their contracts. In exchange, they and everyone they love become less likely to die of cancer.
Most people consider this a good trade. Defense contractors are still doing the math.

The Dead Capital Problem (Or: Why Bombs Are the Worst Investment Since Tulips).

Here's something nobody tells you: when countries "invest" in military hardware, they're not buying assets. They're buying very expensive paperweights that occasionally explode.

A bridge.

It generates economic activity daily (trucks move, people commute, value flows).

It lasts fifty to one hundred years.

It makes everyone richer.

It is pretty to look at.

A missile.

It sits in a hole doing nothing.

It costs one point five million dollars a year just to keep it from rotting.

It can only destroy things (including itself).

It makes everyone nervous.

This is what economists call "dead capital," resources trapped in a form that can't generate returns. It's like if you converted your house into a pile of T.N.T. Sure, it's impressive.

But you can't live in it, rent it out, or do anything except eventually blow it up, at which point you have neither a house nor T.N.T.

As President Eisenhower (a five-star General who knew about killing professionally) put it:

*"Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed, those who are cold and are not clothed."*

He literally ran the biggest military in history, and even HE thought you were being ridiculous about this.

A one percent treaty doesn't ask you to spend twenty-seven point two billion dollars curing diseases. It asks you to redirect twenty-seven point two billion dollars from the least useful one percent of your military budget to things that make you richer and less dead.

It's not spending. It's stopping wasting.

Avoiding Treaty Pitfalls.

Reservation Games.

Countries love to sign treaties then add exceptions that gut them. It's like joining a gym but reserving the right to never go.

Solution: No reservations permitted. All or nothing. Take it or leave it.

Ratification Delays.

Sign with fanfare, take a photo, then let it die in committee forever. Your species calls this "diplomacy.".

Solution: Provisional application. Takes effect on signature. Ratification just formalizes what already started.

Creative Accounting.

Reclassify "military" spending as "peacekeeping" or "homeland security" to dodge obligations.

Solution: Incentive Alignment Bonds, or I.A.B.s, make creative accounting self-defeating.

Politicians who benefit from treaty compliance have personal financial incentive to ensure their nation's full one percent contribution flows through.

Reclassifying military spending to dodge obligations reduces I.A.B. returns for that nation's own legislators, the same people who control budget classifications.

Parliamentary Obstacles.

Executive signs, legislature refuses to implement.

Solution: The treaty obligates the percentage; Congress implements via the annual National Defense Authorization Act (N.D.A.A.).

After Medellin versus Texas (two thousand and eight), the U.S. Supreme Court made clear that not all treaties are self-executing, and a treaty directing budget appropriations would collide with Congress's power of the purse.

So the treaty doesn't try to bypass Congress.

It creates a binding international obligation that Congress fulfills through its normal appropriations process, the same way NATO's spending commitments work.

The I.A.B. mechanism ensures legislators have a personal financial incentive to include the one percent line item every year.

In other signatory nations, similar provisions route implementation through existing budget processes.
