A one percent Treaty

You have enough nuclear weapons to kill everyone thirteen times. Here's how to settle for twelve point eight seven times and use the savings on medicine. Controversial, apparently.

Your one percent Treaty is a piece of paper that says every signatory nation redirects one percent of its annual military budget to enable any patient to participate in pragmatic clinical trials for the most promising new treatments.

The Math.

The Problem. The world spends two point seven two trillion dollars a year on military forces.

The Hack. If every nation reduces military spending by one percent *at the same time*, the balance of power remains identical. No country becomes more vulnerable.

The Prize. That one percent, twenty-seven point two billion dollars a year, is redirected to fund clinical trials for every human disease.

For comparison, twenty-seven point two billion dollars is roughly what Americans spend on Valentine's Day, and what humans spend on pet food every three months.

How It Works.

Funding. This creates a twenty-seven point two billion dollar per year revenue stream for the one percent Treaty Fund.

Execution. A network of Decentralized Institutes of Health subsidizes patient participation in a D.F.D.A., or decentralized F.D.A., for clinical trials that are forty-four point one times cheaper than the current model.

Governance. This involves the global population via Wishocracy, with transparent auditable rules and automated fund allocation.

Transparency. All research, data, and spending are published on a public ledger.

This Isn't a Trade-Off.

It's like if everyone in your neighborhood spent their life savings on attack dogs, and then called it a "safe community" because all the dogs are equally vicious.

When every nation builds one percent fewer explosion devices *at the same time*, you get two good things:

**You become safer.** The global "you might all die by accident" temperature drops one percent. Fewer chances of someone pressing the wrong button, which happens more than you'd think.

**You stop dying from stupid things.** That twenty-seven point two billion dollars pays sick people to try treatments instead of waiting for death.

Economists call this a Kaldor-Hicks improvement, which is their way of saying "the winners win more than the losers lose, and we've decided that counts." In practice, some defense contractors lose one percent of their contracts. In exchange, they and everyone they love become less likely to die of cancer.
Most people consider this a good trade. Defense contractors are still doing the math.

The Dead Capital Problem (Or: Why Bombs Are the Worst Investment Since Tulips).

When countries "invest" in military hardware, they're not buying assets. They're buying very expensive paperweights that occasionally explode.

A bridge.

Generates economic activity daily (trucks move, people commute, value flows).

Lasts fifty to one hundred years.

Makes everyone richer.

Pretty to look at.

A missile.

Sits in a hole doing nothing.

Costs one point five million dollars a year just to keep it from rotting.

Can only destroy things (including itself).

Makes everyone nervous.

This is what economists call "dead capital": resources trapped in a form that can't generate returns. It's like converting your house into a pile of T.N.T. Impressive, but you can't live in it, rent it out, or do anything except eventually blow it up, at which point you have neither a house nor T.N.T.

As President Eisenhower (a five-star General who knew about killing professionally) put it:

"Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed, those who are cold and are not clothed."

He literally ran the biggest military in history, and even HE thought you were being ridiculous about this.

A one percent treaty doesn't ask you to spend twenty-seven point two billion dollars curing diseases. It asks you to redirect twenty-seven point two billion dollars from the least useful one percent of your military budget to things that make you richer and less dead.

The Blueprint.

Treaties are pieces of paper that countries sign when they want to pretend they'll behave. Sometimes they work (you haven't had World War Three yet). Sometimes they don't (you've had about two hundred other wars).
But they're the only tool you have for getting countries to do anything together besides kill each other.

The Money Flow.

The one percent Treaty Fund.

Twenty-seven point two billion dollars annually from military budgets flows into the one percent Treaty Fund. An eighty, ten, ten automatic split divides it before any funds reach discretionary spending:

Eighty percent of the funds, totaling twenty-one point eight billion dollars, is allocated to Pragmatic Clinical Trials for patient subsidies, R. and D., and pandemic prep.

Ten percent, or two point seven two billion dollars, goes to VICTORY Incentive Alignment Bond Returns for perpetual investor payments.

The final ten percent, another two point seven two billion dollars, is for Incentive Alignment Bond Political Incentives to provide rewards for supporting legislators.

Notice what this does: it disentangles money from influence. In current government budgets, the people who provide the money (lobbyists, donors) also control where it goes. Money buys influence, influence redirects money, and the loop is invisible. Here, investors get money (ten percent).
Politicians get career incentives (ten percent). Neither group touches the eighty percent that goes to clinical trials. The corruption is capped, transparent, and separated from the public goods funding.

The eighty percent for pragmatic clinical trials is distributed by the actual people of Earth via Wishocracy, not by a committee of twelve people in a conference room who haven't had a new idea since nineteen eighty-seven. Every human gets direct say on funding priorities. Yes, even Florida Man.

What Happens If This Actually Works.

The Endgame.

A one percent treaty isn't the goal. It's the proof of concept. I've watched roughly eight hundred and forty-seven civilizations reach this exact fork in the road; the ones still around all started with something like one percent, and the ones that didn't are geological features now.

Once your species gets a taste of "spending money on things that help instead of things that explode," you'll want more.

The Ratchet Effect.

Once a one percent treaty is signed and the first cures start rolling out, the system becomes more valuable. Every five to ten years, you hold another global vote. "Grandma's dementia is manageable now. Want to try another half-percent?".

More cures lead to more popular support, which leads to more votes for more funding, which leads to more cures. The endgame: countries bragging about their cancer survival rates instead of their missile counts.

For quantitative projections of health and economic impacts, visit impact dot war on disease dot org.

Avoiding Treaty Pitfalls.

Treaties fail in predictable ways. They all fail for the same boring reasons. Here's how this one avoids the common killers:

Reservation Games.

Countries love to sign treaties then add exceptions that gut them. It's like joining a gym but reserving the right to never go.

Solution: No reservations permitted. All or nothing. Take it or leave it.

Ratification Delays.

Sign with fanfare, take a photo, then let it die in committee forever. Your species calls this "diplomacy.".

Solution: Provisional application. Takes effect on signature. Ratification just formalizes what already started.

Creative Accounting.

Reclassify "military" spending as "peacekeeping" or "homeland security" to dodge obligations.

Solution: incentive-alignment bonds, or I.A.B.s, make creative accounting self-defeating. Politicians who benefit from treaty compliance have personal financial incentive to ensure their nation's full one percent contribution flows through.

Reclassifying military spending to dodge obligations reduces I.A.B. returns for that nation's own legislators, the same people who control budget classifications.

Parliamentary Obstacles.

Executive signs, legislature refuses to implement.

Solution: The treaty obligates the percentage; Congress implements via the annual National Defense Authorization Act (N.D.A.A.). After Medellin versus Texas (two thousand and eight), the Supreme Court made clear that not all treaties are self-executing, so the treaty doesn't try to bypass Congress. It creates a binding international obligation that Congress fulfills through its normal appropriations process, the same way NATO's spending commitments work. The I.A.B. mechanism ensures legislators have a personal incentive to include the one percent line item every year.

Summary.

A one percent treaty is a piece of paper that says "Let's spend one percent less on killing and one percent more on not dying.".

That's it. That's the whole thing.

It doesn't end war. It doesn't destroy the military. It doesn't create world peace.

It just moves the FIRST one percent of your murder budget to your survival budget.

If you can't agree on that, if you can't agree that spending one penny less on death and one penny more on life is a good idea, then you deserve whatever's coming.
