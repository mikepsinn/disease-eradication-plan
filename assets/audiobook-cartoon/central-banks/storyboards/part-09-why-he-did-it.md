# Part 9 storyboard: "Why He Did It" and "What He Killed" (the window)

- **Source:** `knowledge/economics/central-banks.qmd`, "Why He Did It" and "What He Killed" through "I was surprised too."
- **The numbers** (cited in the chapter, not parameters):
  - Second World War bonds: 85 million Americans bought $185 billion.
  - Vietnam: paid by printing; inflation of about 2% a year (the chapter's "below the boredom threshold").
  - De Gaulle, 1963 to 1966: 129 flights and 44 boat trips; 3,313 tonnes of French gold. Operation Vide-Gousset ("Empty Pocket").
  - "Everyone else noticed fifty years later."
  - Bretton Woods, 1944: $35 an ounce.
- **What the viewer must leave with:** when a war couldn't be sold, it was printed, and the gold window was the one thing that punished printing, by letting other countries take the gold home.

## Scene 4 · the-gold-window

- id: the-gold-window
- act: What he killed
- transition_in: cut
- cue: "Here is how it worked, physically."
- time: 27:12.8 to 28:13.1 (60.3 s)
- voiceover: "Here is how it worked, physically. Any foreign government could walk up to a window at the Federal Reserve, hand over thirty-five dollars, and receive one ounce of gold. Real gold. The kind you carry out in a box. This meant that if the U.S. printed too many dollars, governments would notice (because prices would drift above thirty-five dollars per ounce on the open market), and they would start showing up at the window, trading paper for metal, and walking out with your gold. The more you printed, the faster your vault emptied. This was not a rule. Rules can be rewritten by the same people who wrote them, usually at two a.m. when nobody is paying attention. This was a physical constraint. Gold is heavy. It leaves the building. You can see the shelves getting bare. The punishment for money-printing was not a sternly worded letter from an oversight committee. It was a foreign government loading your gold onto a cargo plane and flying it home."

- bit: **The window is a meter on the press: every extra crank empties a shelf.** One envoy, $35, one box of gold. Then the press cranks, the price drifts over the dashed line, the queue grows, and the shelves go bare one by one. Payoff: the letter flutters in harmlessly; the plane, loaded with the gold, takes off.
