# Part 30 storyboard: "How the Ledger Ate the Economy"

- **Source:** `knowledge/economics/central-banks.qmd`, "How the Ledger Ate the Economy" (citing Philippon and Reshef 2012, and Volcker in Suskind 2011). The narration is `central-banks.prepared.txt`.
- **The numbers:** none from `_variables.yml`. The narrator's figures, hardcoded in the chapter:
  - Early 1970s: finance was "about four percent" of GDP (Part 28's narration says three percent for the same start; the chart here uses the 4% said in this part).
  - By 2006: finance workers earned a 50% premium over equally educated workers elsewhere; 30% to 50% of that premium is pure rent (Philippon and Reshef).
  - A well-run business spends about 1% of revenue on accounting; the economy spends 8%, "eight times the standard rate".
- **What the viewer must leave with:** finance pays more because it is nearest the printer, not because the work is harder, so the bright people who would build bridges count money instead.

## Scene 2 · bridges-or-bonds

- id: bridges-or-bonds
- act: The fork
- transition_in: cut
- cue: "The financial sector didn't always cost this much"
- time: 85:36.9 to 87:04.4 (87.5 s)
- voiceover: "The financial sector didn't always cost this much. In the early nineteen-seventies, it was about four percent of G.D.P. Finance workers earned roughly the same as everyone else with similar education. A bright graduate from M.I.T. was equally likely to design bridges or trade bonds, because bridges and bonds paid the same. Then Nixon cut the dollar from gold, regulations were stripped away, and something interesting happened: by two thousand and six, finance workers earned a fifty percent premium over equally educated workers in every other sector. Not because the job got harder. (The job is still arithmetic.) Because the money printer printed money, the money went to banks first, and the banks used some of it to hire away every bright person who might otherwise have done something useful. Philippon and Reshef, two economists who actually measured this, found that thirty to fifty percent of the finance wage premium is pure rent, meaning excess pay not explained by skill, education, or complexity. The financial sector pays more not because it's harder, but because it's closer to the printer. Paul Volcker, who ran the Federal Reserve and therefore knew exactly what he was talking about, summarized the result: "We spent several decades not producing many civil engineers and producing a huge number of financial engineers. And the result is shitty bridges and a shitty financial system"."
- layer: B, plate the-fork
- bit: **Graduates come to a fork, bridges or bonds; once the printer's money pours into the bank first, the bank nets every one of them, and the bridge never gets finished.** Payoff: Volcker says it, and the bridge and the bank both fall down.
